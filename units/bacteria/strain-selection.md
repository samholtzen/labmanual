# *E. coli* strain selection

The diversity of *E. coli* strains, like the diversity of bacteria writ large, is immense and a bit daunting. That said, for most applications, you will be using one or two strains of *E. coli* for your daily work. Below are two molecular biology techniques and the bacteria they use: cloning and protein expression.

## Cloning

Since DNA encodes the instructions of life, manipulating and altering DNA sequences is the first step to engineering biology. To do this, we undertake [molecular cloning](/units/cloning/index.md), a process by which we shuffle, cut, paste, edit, and delete sequences in a molecule of DNA. *In vitro*, we can edit DNA sequences very efficiently, but the yields of fully-formed plasmid DNA are inadequate for most *in vivo* applications. Instead, we use bacteria to synthesize and propagate plasmid DNA to improve our yield.

An ideal host bacteria has the following attributes: (i) it is easily transformed with a plasmid of interest, (ii) it grows quickly at close to ambient temperatures, (iii) it is not pathogenic, and (iv) its growth media is cheap and readily accessible. Our friend *E. coli* meets all of these criteria. Extensive engineering has gone into developing strains of the bacteria that are more amenable to hosting and replicating plasmids than their wild-type counterparts.

Wild-type *E. coli* contains several genes that make it natively inhospitable to harboring and replicating plasmids effectively. Two of the genes that reduce plasmid yield are *recA* (a recombinase that natively fixes DNA damage, but can integrate the plasmid into the bacterial genome instead of propagating it) and *endA* (an endonuclease that generates a double stranded break and degrades plasmid DNA before it can be propagated). Deletion of these genes increases plasmid yields, as more cells retain the plasmid and can therefore propagate it. Deleting these two genes yields the strain [**DH5𝜶**](/units/glossary.md#dh5α), the most common *E. coli* strain used for cloning.
