# Culturing *E. coli* in both liquid and solid substrates

## Liquid culture

There are as many liquid culture media varieties as there are bacterial strains, each offering different advantages and disadvantages. Broadly, media for *E. coli* is separated into three types: culture media, transport media, and minimal media, each chemically different from the others.

### Culture media

For routine culture of bacterial cells, a rich, [**chemically-undefined**](/units/glossary.md#chemically-undefined) media is sufficient. An example of this is lysogeny broth (LB), a rich culture media composed of tryptone (casein proteins digested with protease to yield small peptides), yeast extract, and sodium chloride. Since tryptone and yeast extract are complex, and the composition can vary between tubes, samples, and batches, LB would be considered a chemically-undefined media.

### Minimal media

For culture of bacteria under strict conditions, such as when growth is dependent on the concentration of one or a few very common compounds, researchers use a [**chemically defined**](/units/glossary.md#chemically-defined), or "minimal" media. This allows researchers to control the presence or amount of a common compound (such as a specific amino acid or nucleotide) during the growth process. For *E. coli*, M9 minimal media is standard. It only contains salts (sodium, potassium, phosphate, chloride, magnesium, calcium), ammonium, and a carbon source such as glucose.

### Transport media

Researchers rarely want to keep bacterial cells in culture for extended periods of time, so a storage media is used for freezing bacterial cultures. The composition of this media varies, but it usually contains a bacterial culture in [**log phase**](/units/glossary.md#log-phase) and an anti-ice agent like glycerol at 15-25% (v/v). This prevents ice crystals from forming in bacterial cells and destroying their membranes and allows bacteria to be frozen at -80 ºC for years.

| Media | Chemically defined | Purpose | Type |
| -- | -- | -- | -- |
| Lysogeny broth (LB) | No | Routine culture of bacterial cells | Culture media |
| M9 | Yes | Growth of bacteria where composition of the media must be controlled, or one component must be left out | Minimal media |
| Freeze media | Sometimes | Long-term storage of bacterial cultures | Transport media |

## Solid culture

To grow bacteria in bulk, liquid culture is sufficient. Isolating a single [**clone**](/units/glossary.md#clone) is more difficult and requires changing the scaffold of the media. To accomplish separation of single clones of bacteria, researchers use bacterial plates, composed of their media of choice and the polysaccharide agar. When combined with water, agar forms a hydrogel that allows for bacterial growth on its surface and *minimal movement* which aids in isolation of single bacterial clones.

## The bacterial culture life cycle

During introductory science classes, you may have learned about the logistic curve that governs growth and carrying capacity of an ecosystem. Starting with a small number of reproducing organisms, the population will grow exponentially as one organism becomes two (or more). After some time, the carrying capacity of the ecosystem is reached, and the organisms plateau, the population hovering around this predefined capacity. With a few caveats, the logistic curve models quite well how bacteria (the organisms) grow in a culture (the ecosystem). The bacterial life cycle occurs in the following steps:

1. [**Lag phase**](/units/glossary.md#lag-phase): just after inoculation, cells are adjusting to their new growth media and are not dividing. Instead, they are synthesizing new proteins and preparing to divide.
2. [**Log phase**](/units/glossary.md#log-phase): after some time, the population of bacteria begins to grow at an exponential rate. In order to plot this rate linearly, we take the [**logarithm**](/units/glossary.md#logarithm) of the population at each time point, thus the name [**log phase**](/units/glossary.md#log-phase).
3. [**Stationary phase**](/units/glossary.md#stationary-phase): as nutrients run out, the population reaches the carrying capacity and plateaus. The bacteria begin avoiding division and instead divert resources to stress tolerance.
4. [**Death phase**](/units/glossary.md#death-phase): lack of nutrients, coupled with toxic waste products like organic acids, begins to take their toll on the bacteria, which begin to die.

Working with bacteria is a balance of timing: harvest bacteria too early, and there will not be enough material to work with. Harvest them too late, and the shift toward stress tolerance makes them much less amenable to transformation or engineering. If you are working with bacteria for the first time, be aware that, although they are quite robust organisms, timing is important and should be optimized.
