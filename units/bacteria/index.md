# Bacteria

Bacteria are the prokaryotic workhorses of the wet lab. The most common species of bacteria used for routine techniques such as plasmid cloning and protein expression is *Escherichia coli* (*E. coli*), with countless others harnessed for their unique chemistry and thus their ability to carry out biocatalysis. For the sake of this practical guide, we will focus only on *E. coli*.

## *E. coli*

*Escherichia coli*, or more commonly referred to as *E. coli* is the most common bacteria for routine lab use. It is a [**facultative anaerobe**](/units/glossary.md#facultative-anaerobe), meaning it can tolerate anaerobic environments such as the human digestive tract. It is a [**Gram negative**](/units/glossary.md#gram-negative) bacteria, which means it does not retain Gram stain, and it is flagellated (although some strains are engineered to remove flagellar genes). Their growth is optimal at 37 ºC in oxic conditions and survives on a large number of substrates.

### *E. coli* domestication

The most common laboratory strain of *E. coli* was isolated from a convalescent patient with diphtheria and was named [**K-12**](/units/glossary.md#k-12). K-12 originally possessed an [**F-pilus**](/units/glossary.md#f-pilus) gene (allowing for sharing of genetic material), and the temperate (lysogenic) [**𝞴-phage**](/units/glossary.md#λ-phage), making it difficult to reliably use as a genetic workhorse. After removing the F-pilus and its phage infection, it was renamed MG1655 and has become ubiquitous in molecular biology laboratories. Over time, MG1655 was engineered extensively to fill specific niches in the lab, allowing it to perform well in tasks such as plasmid cloning and protein engineering.
