# Aseptic technique

Bacteria are all around us: on our skin, on the lab benches, and inside plastic and glassware. Propagating a [**monoculture**](/units/glossary.md#monoculture) of E. coli has become a routine task in the wet lab, but requires some special procedures to ensure cultures are not contaminated. This is called [**aseptic technique**](/units/glossary.md#aseptic-technique) and is a staple not only of bacterial cell culture, but mammalian cell culture as well. At its core, aseptic technique involves a series of practices and procedures that acts to minimize contamination of a bacterial culture with undesired organisms like fungi, viruses, bacteriophages, and other bacteria. The principles and application of aseptic technique varies with the context, but often uses [**disinfectants**](/units/glossary.md#disinfectant) (like 70% ethanol, bleach, or isopropanol), aseptic fields generated by apparatuses like [**laminar flow hoods**](/units/glossary.md#laminar-flow-hood) or [**Bunsen burners**](/units/glossary.md#bunsen-burner), and equipment like [**autoclaves**](/units/glossary.md#autoclave) to sterilize tools.

## Sterilization of tools

We prepare all implements like glassware, plastic, and liquid and solid media for laboratory use through the process of [**sterilization**](/units/glossary.md#sterilization). The most common way of sterilization in a molecular biology lab is the [**autoclave**](/units/glossary.md#autoclave), which exposes contents loaded into the chamber to high heat and pressure (121 ºC and 1 atm above ambient) for 15-60 minutes. This is enough to destroy almost all (*almost*) bacteria, parasites, and viruses that are living on a sample. Autoclaves are routinely used to sterilize media like LB, glassware like culture flasks, and plastic like micropipette tips.

Occasionally, media too delicate to be sterilized using the high temperature and pressure of an autoclave is sterilized using filtration. Most bacteria are larger than 200 nm, so filters with a pore size of smaller than 200 nm are used to remove contaminants from the media. This sterilizes media without subjecting it to high temperatures and pressures.

Sometimes, delicate instruments or surfaces that cannot be autoclaved and require a higher level of disinfection than ethanol or bleach can provide. These instead are sterilized using high-intensity UV-A light. This will destroy bacterial and viral DNA and proteins and render them non-infectious.

## Disinfection

To reduce the risk of contamination from surfaces like the bench and hands, we use disinfectants to render bacteria and other microbes nonviable. The most common antibacterial agent used on surfaces is 70% ethanol, which is composed of (you guessed it) 70% ethanol by volume in water. This concentration is speculated to have higher penetration than lower or higher concentrations of ethanol, but few studies have systematically identified this as the optimal concentration. For most purposes, though, it suffices. Isopropanol is also used in place of ethanol at the same concentration.

While bacteria are killed by ethanol, their components (DNA, RNA, protein) are not destroyed completely. Further, viral RNA and DNA are often stabilized by ethanol and isopropanol. If contamination with foreign DNA or RNA is a procedural worry, we use a solution of 10% (v/v) bleach (sodium hypochlorite). Instead of simply disrupting the cellular and chemical 3D structure, this solution will oxidize DNA, RNA and protein, rendering it inactive.

## The aseptic field

A disinfected field does not remain sterile for long; surfaces are quickly colonized by bacteria floating in the air and inhabiting skin cells that are shed over time. In order to maintain the *longevity* of the aseptic field, we use equipment like laminar flow hoods or Bunsen burners to replace potentially contaminated air with heat or HEPA filters across the work surface. For bench work, [**Bunsen burners**](/units/glossary.md#bunsen-burner) offer a small but reliable aseptic field to work in. The heat from the active flame forces air up and out instead of down, removing the possibility of contamination from above. For areas larger than 100 square centimeters, [**laminar flow hoods**](/units/glossary.md#laminar-flow-hood) are used. These pieces of equipment maintain a separation between the outside and inside by fast-moving HEPA-sterilized air.
